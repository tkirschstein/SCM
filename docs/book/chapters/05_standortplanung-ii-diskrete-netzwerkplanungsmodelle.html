6.1 From continuous to discrete facility location planning

In the previous chapter you learnt how to use the centre-of-gravity approach and the Weiszfeld algorithm to determine an arbitrary point in the plane that minimises the weighted distances to a set of demand points. In the example of SushiFresh GmbH – a regional producer of fresh sushi boxes in the Rhine-Main area that supplies six supermarket stores every day – the optimal point calculated in this way lay between Raunheim and Frankfurt, shifted clearly towards Frankfurt, the store with the highest demand. This continuous view of location choice is analytically elegant but of limited help in business practice: SushiFresh cannot build a production facility at exactly the calculated coordinates but has to choose among the commercial plots, halls or existing properties that are actually available. Planning law, land prices, transport links and food hygiene requirements restrict the set of realistically usable locations to a finite set of alternatives known in advance. This is precisely the starting point of discrete facility location planning: instead of an optimisation problem over the entire plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\), the focus is on a selection decision from a fixed list of potential locations.

In Chapter 5 the location question for SushiFresh was already addressed twice: first, a qualitative assessment of three candidate locations was carried out using the AHP (Analytic Hierarchy Process) – with the result that Mainz-Hechtsheim came out narrowly ahead of Raunheim, although the criteria weights were based on judgements that were not entirely consistent. Subsequently, the centre-of-gravity/Weiszfeld approach delivered a continuous optimal point in the middle of the sales area. This chapter poses the location question a third time – now on the basis of hard cost figures: fixed costs for operating a location and variable transport costs to the stores supplied. As we shall see, a purely cost-based, discrete analysis arrives at a different result from the qualitative pre-selection – and precisely this tension between the two methods is discussed explicitly at the end of the chapter.

Definition: Discrete facility location planning

In discrete facility location planning, the subset of a finite set of predefined potential locations is selected that minimises a given target value (usually the sum of fixed location costs and variable transport costs). In contrast to continuous facility location planning, the solution space is no longer continuous but combinatorial: every possible subset of locations represents a feasible solution.

Discrete location decisions are always embedded in a larger supply chain network, which can be roughly divided into the stages of procurement, production, distribution and sales. At each of these stages, nodes (supplier plants, production facilities, warehouses, cross-docking points) can be opened, closed or connected with one another; in this context one also speaks of network design. The lecture emphasises that in practice usually only a section of the overall network is considered: specific locations are selected at one or two stages while the remaining stages are held fixed. Accordingly, this chapter concentrates on the question of which production and distribution locations SushiFresh should operate and which store should be supplied from which location. The models presented can, however, be transferred directly to other network stages.

We approach discrete facility location planning in two steps. First, we consider the special case in which the locations are already fixed and only the delivery quantities have to be optimally allocated to the stores – the classical transportation problem. We then extend the model to include the actual location decision: the warehouse location problem (WLP), in which both the question “where?” and the question “how much to where?” have to be answered simultaneously.

6.3 The classical transportation problem

Let us first assume that SushiFresh – hypothetically, as a starting scenario before the actual location decision – already operates all five locations simultaneously and “only” has to decide which store is supplied from which location and with what quantity. The locations have limited daily production capacities which in total correspond exactly to the total demand of all six stores:

Code
angebot   <- c(S1 = 200, S2 = 150, S3 = 250, S4 = 120, S5 = 125)   # boxes/day
nachfrage <- setNames(filialen_fall$mu, filialen_fall$ID)          # boxes/day

c(Supply = sum(angebot), Demand = sum(nachfrage))
Supply Demand 
   845    845

Five sources (supply of the locations) and six sinks (demand of the stores) – the classical transportation problem.

Definition: Transportation problem

In the (classical) transportation problem, \(m\) sources \(i \in I\) with supply quantities \(s_i\) and \(n\) sinks \(j \in J\) with demand quantities \(a_j\) are given. For each connection \((i,j)\), transport costs \(c_{ij}\) are incurred per unit transported. The task is to find a cost-minimal allocation of the supply quantities to the demand quantities. The problem is called balanced if \(\sum_i s_i = \sum_j a_j\) holds; otherwise it is unbalanced.

Formally, the corresponding linear optimisation model with the transport quantities \(x_{ij} \geq 0\) as decision variables reads:

\[ \begin{align} \min \quad & \sum_{i \in I} \sum_{j \in J} c_{ij} \cdot x_{ij} \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_{j \in J} x_{ij} \leq s_i & \forall i \in I \quad &\rightarrow \text{supply constraint} \\ & \sum_{i \in I} x_{ij} = a_j & \forall j \in J \quad &\rightarrow \text{meet demand exactly} \\ & x_{ij} \geq 0 & \forall (i,j) &\quad \rightarrow \text{non-negativity} \end{align} \]

Since all constraints and the objective function are linear in \(x_{ij}\), this is a pure linear optimisation problem (not an MILP) – the optimal solution always lies at a vertex of the solution polyhedron, and specialised, very efficient solution methods exist.

In depth (theory): Why transportation problems yield integer solutions “by themselves”

The constraint matrix of the transportation problem has a special structure: each variable \(x_{ij}\) appears in exactly two constraints – once in the supply row of \(i\) and once in the demand row of \(j\) – each time with the coefficient 1. Matrices with this structure are totally unimodular, i.e. every square submatrix has the determinant 0, +1 or −1. By Cramer’s rule it follows that every vertex of the solution polyhedron is integer, provided the supply and demand quantities are integer. In practice this means: although the transportation problem is solved as an LP with continuous variables, one automatically obtains integer transport quantities – there is no need to round “half boxes” afterwards.

A second useful property concerns the number of positive transport quantities: a basic solution of a balanced transportation problem has at most \(m + n - 1\) positive variables. With five locations and six stores, at most ten of the thirty possible connections are therefore used – most stores are supplied by exactly one location, and only a few are “split” between two locations. The opening procedures below generate precisely such basic solutions.

Historically, so-called tableau methods were developed for transportation problems; they build on the simplex idea but exploit the special structure. Two classical opening procedures that construct an initial feasible solution are the north-west corner method and Vogel’s approximation method.

The north-west corner method is extremely simple: one starts in the “north-west corner” of the tableau (row 1, column 1), supplies as much as possible there, deletes the row or column thereby exhausted and repeats the procedure for the remaining matrix. Its great advantage is its simplicity; its drawback: the costs \(c_{ij}\) are not taken into account at all when choosing the cells. The method therefore always delivers a feasible, but often economically poor, starting solution.

Vogel’s approximation method (VAM) improves on this by giving priority in each step to the row or column where a “wrong” decision would be most expensive. To do this, a penalty cost difference (penalty) is calculated for each row and each column: the difference between the two lowest costs in that row or column. A large difference means: if the cheapest cell is not chosen here, it becomes disproportionately expensive (opportunity cost of the second-best choice). The basic idea can be summarised as follows:

# Core idea of Vogel's approximation method (simplified):
# Repeat as long as supply and demand remain open:
#   1. For each active row/column, calculate the penalty cost difference
#      = difference between the two lowest costs in that
#        row or column
#   2. Choose the row/column with the LARGEST penalty cost difference
#   3. In it, supply the cheapest cell with the maximum possible
#      quantity (= min(remaining supply, remaining demand))
#   4. Update remaining supply/demand; if a row/column is
#      exhausted, it is removed from further consideration

6.4 The warehouse location problem

In practice, it usually has to be clarified not only how stores are supplied but also from which locations deliveries should be made at all. For SushiFresh this is the actual strategic question: is it worth operating several locations simultaneously, or is consolidation onto one or two locations cheaper? Every location opening entails a fixed annual cost burden, regardless of how much is later actually delivered via this location. This decision problem is called the warehouse location problem (WLP) or also the uncapacitated facility location problem if – as here – no capacity limits per location are assumed.

Definition: Warehouse location problem

Let there be a set of potential locations \(i \in I\) with annual opening costs \(f_i\) and a set of customer locations \(j \in J\) with demand quantities \(a_j\). For each possible transport connection \((i,j) \in E \subseteq I \times J\), transport costs \(c_{ij}\) are incurred per unit supplied. The task is to find the subset of locations to be opened and the corresponding assignment of customers to locations that minimises the sum of opening and transport costs.

In contrast to the transportation problem, the WLP now contains two types of decision variables: a binary location decision \(y_i \in \{0,1\}\) (location \(i\) is opened or not) and, as before, the continuous transport quantities \(x_{ij} \geq 0\). The complete model reads:

\[ \min \rightarrow \sum_{i \in I} y_i \cdot f_i + \sum_{(i,j) \in E} x_{ij} \cdot c_{ij} \]

subject to the constraints

\[ \begin{align} \sum_{(i,j) \in E} x_{ij} &= a_j & \forall j \in J &\quad\rightarrow \text{satisfy demand completely} \\ x_{ij} &\leq y_i \cdot a_j & \forall (i,j) \in E &\quad\rightarrow \text{only transports from opened locations} \\ x_{ij} &\geq 0 & \forall (i,j) \in E &\quad\rightarrow \text{transport quantities non-negative} \\ y_i &\in \{0,1\} & \forall i \in I &\quad\rightarrow \text{location is opened or not} \end{align} \]

The second constraint is the decisive difference from the pure transportation problem: as long as \(y_i = 0\) (location \(i\) not opened), it forces all associated transport quantities \(x_{ij}\) to zero – a closed location cannot supply any store. Only when \(y_i = 1\) is set is \(x_{ij}\) permitted up to at most the full demand \(a_j\). Because both continuous and binary variables appear in the same model, this is a mixed-integer linear optimisation model (mixed-integer linear program, MILP) – a considerably more difficult problem class than the pure LP of the transportation problem.

In depth (theory): Structure of the optimal solution and “strong” formulations

Because the WLP has no capacity limits, its optimal solution has a simple structure: as soon as it is fixed which locations are open, each store is supplied entirely from the cheapest open location. Splitting a store between several locations can never be better, since every box should be transported on the cheapest available connection. The real difficulty of the problem therefore lies solely in the combinatorial selection of the set of locations – with \(|I|\) candidates there are \(2^{|I|}-1\) non-empty subsets. The add and drop heuristics and the helper function .wlp_cost() exploit precisely this observation.

For exact solution with an MILP solver, the formulation of the linking constraint is important. Instead of the disaggregated form \(x_{ij} \leq y_i \cdot a_j\) used here (one constraint per connection), one could also write an aggregated form \(\sum_j x_{ij} \leq y_i \cdot \sum_j a_j\) (one constraint per location). Both formulations have the same integer solutions. Solvers, however, work with the LP relaxation, in which \(y_i\) may take values between 0 and 1. In the aggregated form, a very small \(y_i\) then suffices to open a location “a little” and supply all stores through it – the relaxation provides a weak lower bound, and the branch-and-bound procedure has to search many branches. The disaggregated form, by contrast, requires \(y_i \geq x_{ij}/a_j\) for each individual connection and is therefore much closer to the integer solution; this is referred to as a strong formulation. For the uncapacitated WLP, the LP relaxation of the disaggregated form is often already integer (Daskin 2013).

Unlike the transportation problem in the previous section, we calculate the WLP on an annual basis: the fixed costs \(f_i\) are annual values, and demand must also be scaled up from the daily to the annual level. The numerical example in the lecture uses the simplified factor 1,000 for this (\(a_j = 1{,}000 \cdot \mu_j\)). We adopt this assumption for now and check at the end of the chapter how sensitively the result reacts to a more realistic scaling-up with 365 sales days. R aside: purrr::map() – applying a function to every element

Often one wants to apply the same calculation to every element of a list or vector – for example: “calculate the total costs for every possible combination of locations”. 6.5 Exact solution with ompr

The WLP can be solved exactly as an MILP with specialised solvers without evaluating all subsets individually. In R, the package ompr (Optimization Modeling Package for R) handles the algebraic formulation of the model; the actual numerical solution is delegated via ompr.roi to a solver – here glpk, the free GNU Linear Programming Kit. The appeal of ompr lies in the fact that the objective function and constraints can be translated almost literally from the mathematical formulation into R code.

R aside: Algebraic optimisation modelling with ompr

So far we have solved optimisation problems (centre of gravity, Weiszfeld, Vogel’s method) using tailor-made algorithms. ompr takes a more generic approach: the model is described declaratively (variables, objective function, constraints) and the search for a solution is left to a universal solver. The workflow is divided into six steps:

  1. MIPModel() – create an empty model object,
  2. add_variable() – define decision variables with type (binary, continuous, integer) and index set,
  3. set_objective() – formulate the objective function (sum_expr() forms sums over index sets, analogous to \(\sum\) in mathematical notation),
  4. add_constraint() – formulate constraints, likewise indexed,
  5. solve_model() – pass the model to a specific solver,
  6. get_solution() – extract the optimal values of the decision variables from the result object.

In the following code, y[i] corresponds to the binary location variable \(y_i\) and x[i,j] to the transport quantity variable \(x_{ij}\). sum_expr(f[i] * y[i], i = 1:n_I) forms exactly the expression \(\sum_{i\in I} f_i \cdot y_i\); the two add_constraint() calls implement the demand and linking constraints of the WLP model.

Code
n_J  <- length(nachfrage_jahr)
f    <- fixkosten_vec     # opening costs f_i (EUR/year)
a    <- nachfrage_jahr    # demand a_j (boxes/year)
c_ij <- kosten_mat        # transport costs (EUR/box)

modell <- MIPModel() |>
  # Decision variable y_i: open location i (1) or not (0)
  add_variable(y[i], i = 1:n_I, type = "binary") |>
  # Decision variable x_ij: transport quantity from location i to store j
  add_variable(x[i, j], i = 1:n_I, j = 1:n_J, type = "continuous", lb = 0) |>
  # Objective function: minimise opening costs + transport costs
  set_objective(
    sum_expr(f[i] * y[i], i = 1:n_I) +
      sum_expr(c_ij[i, j] * x[i, j], i = 1:n_I, j = 1:n_J),
    sense = "min") |>
  # C1: satisfy the demand of each store j completely
  add_constraint(sum_expr(x[i, j], i = 1:n_I) == a[j], j = 1:n_J) |>
  # C2: transport only from opened locations (x_ij <= y_i * a_j)
  add_constraint(x[i, j] <= y[i] * a[j], i = 1:n_I, j = 1:n_J)

ergebnis_milp <- solve_model(modell, with_ROI(solver = "glpk", verbose = FALSE))

6.6 Heuristic solution methods: add and drop heuristics

Despite the power of modern MILP solvers, the WLP remains an NP-hard problem: in the worst case, the computing time required for an exact solution grows exponentially with the number of locations and customers. For very large practical instances – a Germany-wide store network with hundreds of candidate locations and thousands of stores, possibly with further constraints (capacities, multi-stage networks) – even a good solver can run into time or memory limits. In such cases, constructive heuristics are often the practicable route to a usable, though not necessarily optimal, solution.

The basic idea of both heuristics presented below is greedy: starting from a feasible initial configuration, in each step exactly the change that reduces total costs the most is made; the procedure stops as soon as no further improvement is possible. Stores are always assigned to the cheapest open location (\(\sigma(j) = \arg\min_{i \in S} c_{ij}\)), so that the total costs of a set of locations \(S\) can be calculated unambiguously as

\[Z(S) = \underbrace{\sum_{i \in S} f_i}_{\text{opening}} + \underbrace{\sum_{j \in J} \min_{i \in S} c_{ij} \cdot a_j}_{\text{transport}}.\]

The add heuristic starts with the empty set \(S = \emptyset\) and in each step adds the location \(i^*\) whose opening reduces total costs the most: \(\Delta_i = Z(S) - Z(S \cup \{i\})\), \(i^* = \arg\max_{i \notin S} \Delta_i\). If \(\Delta_{i^*} > 0\), \(i^*\) is opened; otherwise the procedure terminates. In economic terms, adding a location is worthwhile precisely when the transport cost savings it achieves are greater than its opening costs. Transport costs fall from step to step, while fixed costs rise.

The drop heuristic takes the opposite route: it starts with all locations open (\(S = I\)) and in each step closes the location whose closure reduces total costs the most (\(\Delta_i = Z(S) - Z(S \setminus \{i\})\)), until here too no further improvement is possible. A location is closed if its fixed costs are higher than the transport costs incurred because its stores have to be reassigned to the next-best location.

Start \(S = \emptyset\) (costs \(\infty\)) \(S = I\) (all fixed costs incurred)
Direction constructive (building up) destructive (reducing)
Effort per step \(\mathcal{O}(\lvert I \rvert \cdot \lvert J \rvert)\) \(\mathcal{O}(\lvert I \rvert \cdot \lvert J \rvert)\)

6.9 Summary

This chapter has made the transition from continuous to discrete facility location planning: instead of an arbitrary point in the plane, a subset is sought from a finite list of predefined candidate locations. As a methodological starting point, we became acquainted with the classical transportation problem, in which the supplying locations are already fixed and only the transport flows have to be determined at minimum cost; its constraint matrix is totally unimodular, so that the LP solution is automatically integer. Vogel’s approximation method delivers a good starting solution in a few steps by giving priority to supplying the row or column with the largest penalty cost difference.

The warehouse location problem extends this model to include the actual location decision: a binary variable \(y_i\) per location decides on opening or not opening, linked to the transport quantities \(x_{ij}\) via the constraint \(x_{ij} \leq y_i \cdot a_j\). Because both binary and continuous variables occur, this is an MILP that can be solved exactly with ompr in a few lines of code closely modelled on the mathematical formulation. The SushiFresh example showed that the centrally located Raunheim site forms the backbone of every good network despite having the highest investment costs; whether a second location in Wiesbaden-Erbenheim is added depends – as the sensitivity analysis showed – on the scaling-up of demand. The qualitative AHP recommendation (Mainz-Hechtsheim) was not confirmed by the cost optimisation, which underlines the need for both methods in the decision-making process. Since the WLP is NP-hard, constructive add and drop heuristics are a practicable way out for large instances – in the case example both found the global optimum, although this is not a generally valid property of these methods.

On the R side, this chapter has introduced you to four new building blocks: lists as a flexible container for heterogeneous data and function results; purrr::map()/map_dbl()/map_dfr() for compactly applying a function to every element of a list; matrices as the natural representation of position-based tableaus, with rowSums()/colSums() for consistency checks; and algebraic optimisation modelling with ompr.

## From continuous to discrete facility location planning

In the previous chapter you learnt how to use the centre-of-gravity approach and the Weiszfeld algorithm to determine an *arbitrary* point in the plane that minimises the weighted distances to a set of demand points. In the example of **SushiFresh GmbH** – a regional producer of fresh sushi boxes in the Rhine-Main area that supplies six supermarket stores every day – the optimal point calculated in this way lay between Raunheim and Frankfurt, shifted clearly towards Frankfurt, the store with the highest demand. This continuous view of location choice is analytically elegant but of limited help in business practice: SushiFresh cannot build a production facility at exactly the calculated coordinates but has to choose among the commercial plots, halls or existing properties that are actually available. Planning law, land prices, transport links and food hygiene requirements restrict the set of realistically usable locations to a **finite set of alternatives known in advance**. This is precisely the starting point of **discrete facility location planning**: instead of an optimisation problem over the entire plane $\mathbb{R}^2$, the focus is on a **selection decision** from a fixed list of potential locations.

In Chapter [-@sec-standort-1] the location question for SushiFresh was already addressed twice: first, a qualitative assessment of three candidate locations was carried out using the **AHP** (*Analytic Hierarchy Process*) – with the result that **Mainz-Hechtsheim** came out narrowly ahead of Raunheim, although the criteria weights were based on judgements that were not entirely consistent. Subsequently, the **centre-of-gravity/Weiszfeld approach** delivered a continuous optimal point in the middle of the sales area. This chapter poses the location question a third time – now on the basis of hard **cost figures**: fixed costs for operating a location and variable transport costs to the stores supplied. As we shall see, a purely cost-based, discrete analysis arrives at a different result from the qualitative pre-selection – and precisely this tension between the two methods is discussed explicitly at the end of the chapter.

::: {.definition}
**Definition: Discrete facility location planning**

In discrete facility location planning, the subset of a finite set of predefined potential locations is selected that minimises a given target value (usually the sum of fixed location costs and variable transport costs). In contrast to continuous facility location planning, the solution space is no longer continuous but **combinatorial**: every possible subset of locations represents a feasible solution.
:::

Discrete location decisions are always embedded in a larger **supply chain network**, which can be roughly divided into the stages of procurement, production, distribution and sales. At each of these stages, nodes (supplier plants, production facilities, warehouses, cross-docking points) can be opened, closed or connected with one another; in this context one also speaks of **network design**. The lecture emphasises that in practice usually only a **section** of the overall network is considered: specific locations are selected at one or two stages while the remaining stages are held fixed. Accordingly, this chapter concentrates on the question of which production and distribution locations SushiFresh should operate and which store should be supplied from which location. The models presented can, however, be transferred directly to other network stages.

We approach discrete facility location planning in two steps. First, we consider the **special case** in which the locations are already fixed and only the delivery quantities have to be optimally allocated to the stores – the classical **transportation problem**. We then extend the model to include the actual location decision: the **warehouse location problem (WLP)**, in which both the question “where?” and the question “how much to where?” have to be answered simultaneously.

## The classical transportation problem

Let us first assume that SushiFresh – hypothetically, as a starting scenario *before* the actual location decision – already operates **all five** locations simultaneously and “only” has to decide which store is supplied from which location and with what quantity. The locations have limited daily production capacities which in total correspond exactly to the total demand of all six stores:

```{r}
#| label: ch5-angebot

angebot   <- c(S1 = 200, S2 = 150, S3 = 250, S4 = 120, S5 = 125)   # boxes/day
nachfrage <- setNames(filialen_fall$mu, filialen_fall$ID)          # boxes/day

c(Supply = sum(angebot), Demand = sum(nachfrage))
```

Five **sources** (supply of the locations) and six **sinks** (demand of the stores) – the classical **transportation problem**.

::: {.definition}
**Definition: Transportation problem**

In the (classical) transportation problem, $m$ sources $i \in I$ with supply quantities $s_i$ and $n$ sinks $j \in J$ with demand quantities $a_j$ are given. For each connection $(i,j)$, transport costs $c_{ij}$ are incurred per unit transported. The task is to find a cost-minimal allocation of the supply quantities to the demand quantities. The problem is called **balanced** if $\sum_i s_i = \sum_j a_j$ holds; otherwise it is **unbalanced**.
:::

Formally, the corresponding linear optimisation model with the transport quantities $x_{ij} \geq 0$ as decision variables reads:

$$
\begin{align}
\min \quad & \sum_{i \in I} \sum_{j \in J} c_{ij} \cdot x_{ij} \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_{j \in J} x_{ij} \leq s_i & \forall i \in I \quad &\rightarrow \text{supply constraint} \\
& \sum_{i \in I} x_{ij} = a_j & \forall j \in J \quad &\rightarrow \text{meet demand exactly} \\
& x_{ij} \geq 0 & \forall (i,j) &\quad \rightarrow \text{non-negativity}
\end{align}
$$

Since all constraints and the objective function are linear in $x_{ij}$, this is a pure **linear optimisation problem** (not an MILP) – the optimal solution always lies at a vertex of the solution polyhedron, and specialised, very efficient solution methods exist.

::: {.vertiefung}
**In depth (theory): Why transportation problems yield integer solutions “by themselves”**

The constraint matrix of the transportation problem has a special structure: each variable $x_{ij}$ appears in exactly two constraints – once in the supply row of $i$ and once in the demand row of $j$ – each time with the coefficient 1. Matrices with this structure are **totally unimodular**, i.e. every square submatrix has the determinant 0, +1 or −1. By Cramer's rule it follows that every vertex of the solution polyhedron is integer, provided the supply and demand quantities are integer. In practice this means: although the transportation problem is solved as an LP with continuous variables, one automatically obtains integer transport quantities – there is no need to round “half boxes” afterwards.

A second useful property concerns the number of positive transport quantities: a basic solution of a balanced transportation problem has at most $m + n - 1$ positive variables. With five locations and six stores, at most ten of the thirty possible connections are therefore used – most stores are supplied by exactly one location, and only a few are “split” between two locations. The opening procedures below generate precisely such basic solutions.
:::

Historically, so-called **tableau methods** were developed for transportation problems; they build on the simplex idea but exploit the special structure. Two classical opening procedures that construct an initial feasible solution are the **north-west corner method** and **Vogel's approximation method**.

The north-west corner method is extremely simple: one starts in the “north-west corner” of the tableau (row 1, column 1), supplies as much as possible there, deletes the row or column thereby exhausted and repeats the procedure for the remaining matrix. Its great advantage is its simplicity; its drawback: the costs $c_{ij}$ are **not taken into account at all** when choosing the cells. The method therefore always delivers a feasible, but often economically poor, starting solution.

**Vogel's approximation method (VAM)** improves on this by giving priority in each step to the row or column where a “wrong” decision would be most expensive. To do this, a **penalty cost difference** (*penalty*) is calculated for each row and each column: the difference between the two lowest costs in that row or column. A large difference means: if the cheapest cell is not chosen here, it becomes disproportionately expensive (opportunity cost of the second-best choice). The basic idea can be summarised as follows:

```r
# Core idea of Vogel's approximation method (simplified):
# Repeat as long as supply and demand remain open:
#   1. For each active row/column, calculate the penalty cost difference
#      = difference between the two lowest costs in that
#        row or column
#   2. Choose the row/column with the LARGEST penalty cost difference
#   3. In it, supply the cheapest cell with the maximum possible
#      quantity (= min(remaining supply, remaining demand))
#   4. Update remaining supply/demand; if a row/column is
#      exhausted, it is removed from further consideration
```

::: {.r-exkurs}
**R aside: Matrices as cost and transport tableaus**

So far you have mainly encountered tabular data as a `data.frame`/`tibble`, in which each column represents a named attribute vector with distinct content (e.g. place, longitude, demand). A cost or transport tableau, by contrast, is a **homogeneous, purely numerical table** in which what matters is not column names in the substantive sense but the *position* (row $i$, column $j$). The R data type **matrix** (`matrix()`) is the natural choice for this – as with `kosten_mat` above. Rows and columns can be named via `dimnames` and then addressed directly:

:::

## The warehouse location problem

In practice, it usually has to be clarified not only *how* stores are supplied but also *from which* locations deliveries should be made at all. For SushiFresh this is the actual strategic question: is it worth operating several locations simultaneously, or is **consolidation** onto one or two locations cheaper? Every location opening entails a fixed annual cost burden, regardless of how much is later actually delivered via this location. This decision problem is called the **warehouse location problem (WLP)** or also the **uncapacitated facility location problem** if – as here – no capacity limits per location are assumed.

::: {.definition}
**Definition: Warehouse location problem**

Let there be a set of potential locations $i \in I$ with annual opening costs $f_i$ and a set of customer locations $j \in J$ with demand quantities $a_j$. For each possible transport connection $(i,j) \in E \subseteq I \times J$, transport costs $c_{ij}$ are incurred per unit supplied. The task is to find the subset of locations to be opened and the corresponding assignment of customers to locations that minimises the sum of opening and transport costs.
:::

In contrast to the transportation problem, the WLP now contains **two types of decision variables**: a binary location decision $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ (location $i$ is opened or not) and, as before, the continuous transport quantities $x_{ij} \geq 0$. The complete model reads:

$$
\min \rightarrow \sum_{i \in I} y_i \cdot f_i + \sum_{(i,j) \in E} x_{ij} \cdot c_{ij}
$$

subject to the constraints

$$
\begin{align}
  \sum_{(i,j) \in E} x_{ij} &= a_j          & \forall j \in J      &\quad\rightarrow \text{satisfy demand completely} \\
  x_{ij}                    &\leq y_i \cdot a_j & \forall (i,j) \in E &\quad\rightarrow \text{only transports from opened locations} \\
  x_{ij}                    &\geq 0          & \forall (i,j) \in E &\quad\rightarrow \text{transport quantities non-negative} \\
  y_i                       &\in \{0,1\}     & \forall i \in I     &\quad\rightarrow \text{location is opened or not}
\end{align}
$$

The second constraint is the decisive difference from the pure transportation problem: as long as $y_i = 0$ (location $i$ not opened), it forces *all* associated transport quantities $x_{ij}$ to zero – a closed location cannot supply any store. Only when $y_i = 1$ is set is $x_{ij}$ permitted up to at most the full demand $a_j$. Because both continuous and binary variables appear in the same model, this is a **mixed-integer linear optimisation model** (*mixed-integer linear program*, MILP) – a considerably more difficult problem class than the pure LP of the transportation problem.

::: {.vertiefung}
**In depth (theory): Structure of the optimal solution and “strong” formulations**

Because the WLP has no capacity limits, its optimal solution has a simple structure: as soon as it is fixed which locations are open, each store is supplied **entirely from the cheapest open location**. Splitting a store between several locations can never be better, since every box should be transported on the cheapest available connection. The real difficulty of the problem therefore lies solely in the combinatorial selection of the set of locations – with $|I|$ candidates there are $2^{|I|}-1$ non-empty subsets. The add and drop heuristics and the helper function `.wlp_cost()` exploit precisely this observation.

For exact solution with an MILP solver, the formulation of the linking constraint is important. Instead of the **disaggregated** form $x_{ij} \leq y_i \cdot a_j$ used here (one constraint per connection), one could also write an **aggregated** form $\sum_j x_{ij} \leq y_i \cdot \sum_j a_j$ (one constraint per location). Both formulations have the same integer solutions. Solvers, however, work with the **LP relaxation**, in which $y_i$ may take values between 0 and 1. In the aggregated form, a very small $y_i$ then suffices to open a location “a little” and supply all stores through it – the relaxation provides a weak lower bound, and the branch-and-bound procedure has to search many branches. The disaggregated form, by contrast, requires $y_i \geq x_{ij}/a_j$ for each individual connection and is therefore much closer to the integer solution; this is referred to as a **strong formulation**. For the uncapacitated WLP, the LP relaxation of the disaggregated form is often already integer [@daskin2013network].
:::

Unlike the transportation problem in the previous section, we calculate the WLP on an **annual basis**: the fixed costs $f_i$ are annual values, and demand must also be scaled up from the daily to the annual level. The numerical example in the lecture uses the simplified factor 1,000 for this ($a_j = 1{,}000 \cdot \mu_j$). We adopt this assumption for now and check at the end of the chapter how sensitively the result reacts to a more realistic scaling-up with 365 sales days. ## Exact solution with ompr

The WLP can be solved exactly as an MILP with specialised solvers without evaluating all subsets individually. In R, the package **`ompr`** (*Optimization Modeling Package for R*) handles the algebraic formulation of the model; the actual numerical solution is delegated via `ompr.roi` to a solver – here `glpk`, the free *GNU Linear Programming Kit*. The appeal of `ompr` lies in the fact that the objective function and constraints can be translated almost literally from the mathematical formulation into R code.

::: {.r-exkurs}
**R aside: Algebraic optimisation modelling with `ompr`**

So far we have solved optimisation problems (centre of gravity, Weiszfeld, Vogel's method) using **tailor-made algorithms**. `ompr` takes a more generic approach: the model is described **declaratively** (variables, objective function, constraints) and the search for a solution is left to a universal solver. The workflow is divided into six steps:

1. `MIPModel()` – create an empty model object,
2. `add_variable()` – define decision variables with type (`binary`, `continuous`, `integer`) and index set,
3. `set_objective()` – formulate the objective function (`sum_expr()` forms sums over index sets, analogous to $\sum$ in mathematical notation),
4. `add_constraint()` – formulate constraints, likewise indexed,
5. `solve_model()` – pass the model to a specific solver,
6. `get_solution()` – extract the optimal values of the decision variables from the result object.

In the following code, `y[i]` corresponds to the binary location variable $y_i$ and `x[i,j]` to the transport quantity variable $x_{ij}$. `sum_expr(f[i] * y[i], i = 1:n_I)` forms exactly the expression $\sum_{i\in I} f_i \cdot y_i$; the two `add_constraint()` calls implement the demand and linking constraints of the WLP model.

```{r}
#| label: ch5-ompr-model

n_J  <- length(nachfrage_jahr)
f    <- fixkosten_vec     # opening costs f_i (EUR/year)
a    <- nachfrage_jahr    # demand a_j (boxes/year)
c_ij <- kosten_mat        # transport costs (EUR/box)

modell <- MIPModel() |>
  # Decision variable y_i: open location i (1) or not (0)
  add_variable(y[i], i = 1:n_I, type = "binary") |>
  # Decision variable x_ij: transport quantity from location i to store j
  add_variable(x[i, j], i = 1:n_I, j = 1:n_J, type = "continuous", lb = 0) |>
  # Objective function: minimise opening costs + transport costs
  set_objective(
    sum_expr(f[i] * y[i], i = 1:n_I) +
      sum_expr(c_ij[i, j] * x[i, j], i = 1:n_I, j = 1:n_J),
    sense = "min") |>
  # C1: satisfy the demand of each store j completely
  add_constraint(sum_expr(x[i, j], i = 1:n_I) == a[j], j = 1:n_J) |>
  # C2: transport only from opened locations (x_ij <= y_i * a_j)
  add_constraint(x[i, j] <= y[i] * a[j], i = 1:n_I, j = 1:n_J)

ergebnis_milp <- solve_model(modell, with_ROI(solver = "glpk", verbose = FALSE))
```
:::

Wiesbaden-Erbenheim supplies only the Wiesbaden store, while all other five stores are supplied from Raunheim – each store entirely from exactly one location, as theory predicts for the uncapacitated WLP. The decisive difference from enumeration is the **method of calculation**: `ompr`/`glpk` did not have to evaluate all 31 subsets individually but internally uses branch-and-bound and cutting-plane methods, with which even WLP instances with hundreds of locations and customers can be solved exactly in reasonable time.

## Heuristic solution methods: add and drop heuristics

Despite the power of modern MILP solvers, the WLP remains an **NP-hard** problem: in the worst case, the computing time required for an exact solution grows exponentially with the number of locations and customers. For very large practical instances – a Germany-wide store network with hundreds of candidate locations and thousands of stores, possibly with further constraints (capacities, multi-stage networks) – even a good solver can run into time or memory limits. In such cases, **constructive heuristics** are often the practicable route to a usable, though not necessarily optimal, solution.

The basic idea of both heuristics presented below is **greedy**: starting from a feasible initial configuration, in each step exactly the change that reduces total costs the most is made; the procedure stops as soon as no further improvement is possible. Stores are always assigned to the *cheapest open* location ($\sigma(j) = \arg\min_{i \in S} c_{ij}$), so that the total costs of a set of locations $S$ can be calculated unambiguously as

$$Z(S) = \underbrace{\sum_{i \in S} f_i}_{\text{opening}} + \underbrace{\sum_{j \in J} \min_{i \in S} c_{ij} \cdot a_j}_{\text{transport}}.$$

The **add heuristic** starts with the empty set $S = \emptyset$ and in each step adds the location $i^*$ whose opening reduces total costs the most: $\Delta_i = Z(S) - Z(S \cup \{i\})$, $i^* = \arg\max_{i \notin S} \Delta_i$. If $\Delta_{i^*} > 0$, $i^*$ is opened; otherwise the procedure terminates. In economic terms, adding a location is worthwhile precisely when the **transport cost savings** it achieves are greater than its **opening costs**. Transport costs fall from step to step, while fixed costs rise.

The **drop heuristic** takes the opposite route: it starts with *all* locations open ($S = I$) and in each step closes the location whose closure reduces total costs the most ($\Delta_i = Z(S) - Z(S \setminus \{i\})$), until here too no further improvement is possible. A location is closed if its fixed costs are higher than the transport costs incurred because its stores have to be reassigned to the next-best location.

| Feature | Add heuristic | Drop heuristic |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Start | $S = \emptyset$ (costs $\infty$) | $S = I$ (all fixed costs incurred) |
| Direction | constructive (building up) | destructive (reducing) |
| Effort per step | $\mathcal{O}(\lvert I \rvert \cdot \lvert J \rvert)$ | $\mathcal{O}(\lvert I \rvert \cdot \lvert J \rvert)$ |
| Tendency | rather few locations | rather more locations |

Both variants are implemented in `R/scm_functions.R` as `add_heuristic_wlp()` and `drop_heuristic_wlp()` respectively (including the internal cost calculation via `.wlp_cost()`). With `verbose = TRUE` they log each step:


## How robust is the solution? A sensitivity analysis

The flat cost curve suggests that the optimal solution could react sensitively to assumptions. The most important assumption of the numerical example is the scaling-up of daily to annual demand by the factor 1,000. If SushiFresh sells on 365 days a year, the actual annual quantity – and hence the weight of transport costs relative to fixed costs – would be considerably smaller. With `map_dfr()` the optimum can be determined for different scaling factors:

```{r}
#| label: ch5-sensitivitaet

bestes_netz <- function(faktor) {
  a_tmp  <- filialen_fall$mu * faktor
  kosten <- map_dbl(alle_teilmengen, ~ .wlp_cost(.x, fixkosten_vec, kosten_mat, a_tmp)$total)
  S      <- alle_teilmengen[[which.min(kosten)]]
  tibble(Days_per_year = faktor,
         Optimal_network = paste(standorte_fall$Standort[S], collapse = " + "),
         Total_costs = round(min(kosten)))
}

map_dfr(c(250, 300, 365, 500, 750, 1000), bestes_netz) |>
  kbl(format.args = list(big.mark = ",", decimal.mark = ".")) |>
  kable_styling(bootstrap_options = c("striped", "condensed"), full_width = FALSE)
```

The result is instructive: with a realistic scaling-up based on 365 sales days, it is no longer the two-location solution but the **single location Raunheim** that is optimal. The lower the transport volume, the less worthwhile the fixed cost burden of a second location; only at considerably higher annual quantities does the additional proximity to the Wiesbaden store pay off. The sensitivity analysis thus shows that the question “one or two locations?” is finely balanced in the case example – while the role of **Raunheim as the central location** remains stable in all scenarios.

## Qualitative vs. quantitative location assessment

At this point it is worth looking back at Chapter [-@sec-standort-1]: there, the **AHP analysis** narrowly favoured **Mainz-Hechtsheim** among the three original candidates – mainly because of the very good rating for the availability of skilled labour there. The **quantitative cost optimisation** carried out in this chapter comes to a clearly different conclusion: Mainz-Hechtsheim does not appear in any of the best solutions; the cost-optimal choice is Raunheim (alone or together with Wiesbaden-Erbenheim).

```{r}
#| label: ch5-ahp-vs-milp

map_dfr(1:3, function(i) {
  res <- .wlp_cost(i, fixkosten_vec, kosten_mat, nachfrage_jahr)
  tibble(Location = standorte_fall$Standort[i], Fixed_costs = res$fixed,
         Transport_costs = round(res$transport), Total_costs = round(res$total))
}) |>
  kbl(caption = "The three original candidates as single locations (factor 1,000)",
      format.args = list(big.mark = ",", decimal.mark = ".")) |>
  kable_styling(bootstrap_options = c("striped", "condensed"), full_width = FALSE)
```

The reason for the discrepancy lies in the transport costs: Mainz-Hechtsheim lies on the western edge of the sales area, whereas the bulk of demand (above all Frankfurt) arises further east. The transport costs of a single location at Mainz-Hechtsheim are therefore far higher than those of Raunheim – a disadvantage that neither the lower fixed costs nor the good availability of skilled labour can offset, as long as the latter is not valued in euros.

This observation is not a contradiction but illustrates the **different function** of the two methods in the decision-making process: the AHP is suitable for an initial **pre-selection** based on criteria that are difficult to quantify and partly soft, and for incorporating different stakeholder perspectives. It does not, however, replace a robust **cost optimisation** as soon as hard figures are available. Conversely, a pure cost optimisation falls short if aspects that are difficult to monetise are ignored: if hardly any suitable skilled workers could actually be found in Raunheim, this would be reflected in higher personnel costs, quality problems or start-up difficulties of which the WLP knows nothing. In practice, the two methods complement each other: qualitative methods to reduce the search space and to check knock-out criteria, quantitative optimisation models for the final, cost-based decision between the remaining candidates.

## Summary

This chapter has made the transition from continuous to **discrete facility location planning**: instead of an arbitrary point in the plane, a subset is sought from a finite list of predefined candidate locations. As a methodological starting point, we became acquainted with the **classical transportation problem**, in which the supplying locations are already fixed and only the transport flows have to be determined at minimum cost; its constraint matrix is totally unimodular, so that the LP solution is automatically integer. **Vogel's approximation method** delivers a good starting solution in a few steps by giving priority to supplying the row or column with the largest penalty cost difference.

The **warehouse location problem** extends this model to include the actual location decision: a binary variable $y_i$ per location decides on opening or not opening, linked to the transport quantities $x_{ij}$ via the constraint $x_{ij} \leq y_i \cdot a_j$. Because both binary and continuous variables occur, this is an MILP that can be solved exactly with `ompr` in a few lines of code closely modelled on the mathematical formulation. The SushiFresh example showed that the centrally located Raunheim site forms the backbone of every good network despite having the highest investment costs; whether a second location in Wiesbaden-Erbenheim is added depends – as the sensitivity analysis showed – on the scaling-up of demand. The qualitative AHP recommendation (Mainz-Hechtsheim) was not confirmed by the cost optimisation, which underlines the need for both methods in the decision-making process. Since the WLP is NP-hard, constructive **add** and **drop heuristics** are a practicable way out for large instances – in the case example both found the global optimum, although this is not a generally valid property of these methods.

On the R side, this chapter has introduced you to four new building blocks: **lists** as a flexible container for heterogeneous data and function results; **`purrr::map()`/`map_dbl()`/`map_dfr()`** for compactly applying a function to every element of a list; **matrices** as the natural representation of position-based tableaus, with `rowSums()`/`colSums()` for consistency checks; and **algebraic optimisation modelling** with `ompr`.
