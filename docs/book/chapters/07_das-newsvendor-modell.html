8.1 Single-period decisions under uncertainty

In Chapter 7 we learned how demand uncertainty can be described with probability distributions and how to make statements about normally distributed random variables using rnorm(), dnorm(), pnorm() and qnorm(). The principle of Monte Carlo simulation with replicate() and the empirical distribution function ecdf() were also introduced there. In this chapter we use exactly this toolkit to solve one of the central decision situations of supply chain management: the question of how much of a product should be ordered or produced when demand only becomes known after the decision and the quantity can no longer be corrected during the selling period.

This problem is called the newsvendor model (also newsboy problem or single-period inventory model), named after the classic example of a newspaper seller who has to decide every morning how many copies of a daily newspaper to buy before knowing the actual demand of the day. The mathematical structure of the problem was formally studied as early as the 1950s by Arrow et al. (1951) and has since been one of the most frequently used models in inventory planning (Cachon and Terwiesch 2024; Silver et al. 2017). All applications share the following characteristics:

  • A single order or production decision is made before the start of the selling period.
  • Within the period no reordering is possible — supply and demand only finally meet at the end of the period.
  • Demand in the period is uncertain at the time of the decision, but its distribution is known (e.g. estimated from historical sales data).
  • Unsold units have a lower value at the end of the period than originally calculated (discount sale, disposal, return to the manufacturer) — and ordering too few units leads to lost contribution margin.

This makes the newsvendor model the microeconomic foundation for much more far-reaching questions: How high should the safety stock be in a multi-period warehouse? How large should the capacity of a production line be? How many rooms should a hotel offer for a particular night? We will see in this chapter that all these questions essentially share the same formal structure.

8.2 Case example: sushi boxes in the supermarket

For illustration, we accompany the entire chapter with a running example: SushiFresh GmbH, a regional producer of fresh sushi boxes in the Rhine-Main area, supplies several supermarket stores every day with freshly produced sushi boxes. The timeline of a selling day looks as follows for each store:

  1. 7 a.m.: The sushi boxes are freshly prepared and delivered to the respective store before opening.
  2. 8 a.m.: Sales in the supermarket begin. Replenishment during the day is ruled out — the quantity produced is fixed for the whole day.
  3. 7 p.m.: Unsold boxes are collected again by SushiFresh.
  4. 8 p.m.: The leftover goods are sold at a discount, donated or disposed of — regular sale on the following day is no longer permitted for food safety reasons.

The central business decision is made in the morning, long before the actual daily demand of the respective store is known: How many sushi boxes should SushiFresh produce per store?

8.2.1 Revenue and cost structure

The following simplified values apply to a single sushi box:

Quantity Meaning Value
Selling price \(p\) Revenue per sushi box sold €10
Production and delivery costs \(c\) Costs per prepared sushi box €5
Salvage value \(v\) Revenue from evening discount or disposal €2

For economically meaningful applications of the model, \(p > c > v\) must always hold: if the salvage value \(v\) were not below the production cost \(c\), there would be no incentive to limit the order quantity at all — one could produce any amount, since even unsold goods would still be recovered at least at cost. Conversely, if the selling price \(p\) were not above the production cost \(c\), selling would not be worthwhile in principle.

From these three quantities we derive two central cost categories that will appear again and again throughout the chapter:

Definition: underage and overage costs

The underage costs \(c^u\) (underage cost) correspond to the lost contribution margin per unit that could not be sold because too little was ordered: \[c^u = p - c\]

The overage costs \(c^o\) (overage cost) correspond to the loss per unit that was not sold at the end of the period: \[c^o = c - v\]

For our sushi example this gives: \(c^u = 10 - 5 = 5\ \text{€}\) and \(c^o = 5 - 2 = 3\ \text{€}\).

Both quantities are opportunity costs — no money in the amount of \(c^u\) or \(c^o\) actually flows out; rather, they represent forgone or lost contribution margin compared with the best possible decision in each case. Precisely this tension between ordering “too little” and “too much” is the core of the newsvendor problem.

8.3 Deriving the profit function

If we denote the (random) daily demand by \(D\) and the order quantity set by the supplier by \(q\), two situations can be distinguished.

Case 1 — overage (\(q > D\)): More is produced than demanded. Exactly \(D\) units are sold, while \(q - D\) units must be recovered at the salvage value. The contribution margin is \[P(D,q) = D \cdot c^u - (q - D) \cdot c^o.\]

Case 2 — underage (\(q < D\)): Less is produced than demanded. Since no more goods are available than were produced, exactly \(q\) units are sold — the excess demand \(D - q\) remains unserved and the associated contribution margin is lost. We have \[P(D,q) = q \cdot c^u.\]

Using the positive part function \((x)^+ := \max(x,0)\), both cases can be combined into a single profit function valid for any values of \(D\) and \(q\): \[ P(D,q) = c^u \cdot \min(D,q) - c^o \cdot (q-D)^+. \]

Since \(\min(D,q) = D - (D-q)^+\) holds (the quantity sold equals demand minus the unserved shortage), the profit function can equivalently be written as: \[ P(D,q) = \underbrace{D \cdot c^u}_{\text{maximum possible CM}} - \underbrace{(D-q)^+ \cdot c^u}_{\text{shortage costs}} - \underbrace{(q-D)^+ \cdot c^o}_{\text{overstock costs}}. \]

This notation makes the economic logic particularly clear: starting from the largest possible contribution margin \(D \cdot c^u\), which would be achieved if demand were met exactly, two types of cost are deducted — the shortage costs when the order quantity is too low and the overstock costs when it is too high. Since \(D\) is a random variable at the time of the decision, we consider the expected value over all possible demand realisations: \[ E[P(q)] = \mu \cdot c^u - c^u \cdot E\big[(D-q)^+\big] - c^o \cdot E\big[(q-D)^+\big], \] where \(\mu = E[D]\) is the expected demand. Since the first term \(\mu \cdot c^u\) does not depend on \(q\), maximising the expected profit is equivalent to minimising the expected shortage and overstock costs: \[ \min_q\ E[C(q)] = c^u \cdot E\big[(D-q)^+\big] + c^o \cdot E\big[(q-D)^+\big]. \]

Case study: expected contribution margin for \(q = 40\) boxes (calculation from the lecture)

Before the optimisation is solved in general, it is worth working through a single production quantity by hand. For a small store, SushiFresh estimates four demand scenarios (30, 40, 50 or 60 boxes with probabilities 0.20 / 0.35 / 0.30 / 0.15). If \(q = 40\) boxes are produced, each scenario yields:

Demand \(D\) \(P(D=d)\) Overstock \((q-D)^+\) Shortage \((D-q)^+\) \(P(D,q) = c^u\min(D,q) - c^o (q-D)^+\)
30 0.20 10 0 \(5\cdot 30 - 3\cdot 10 = 120\) €
40 0.35 0 0 \(5\cdot 40 = 200\) €
50 0.30 0 10 \(5\cdot 40 = 200\) €
60 0.15 0 20 \(5\cdot 40 = 200\) €

The expected contribution margin is therefore \(E[P \mid q=40] = 0.20\cdot 120 + 0.35\cdot 200 + 0.30\cdot 200 + 0.15\cdot 200 = 184\) €.

Before we solve this optimisation formally, we implement the model for the discrete case ourselves in R — this sharpens understanding of the formula far more than merely reading it.

R aside: encapsulating a model function

In Chapter 2 you learned how to write your own functions with function(). So far these were mostly short helper functions with a few lines. For more complex models — such as the newsvendor model — it is worth encapsulating the entire calculation scheme in a single, well-documented function: input parameters go in, all intermediate quantities are calculated, and at the end a structured result (typically a list()) is returned. The function newsvendor_normal() in R/scm_functions.R, which we will use later in this chapter, is built according to exactly this pattern.

We now build the discrete variant ourselves step by step:

Code
library(tidyverse)  # dplyr, tibble, ggplot2, purrr

#' Newsvendor model with discrete demand
#'
#' Calculates, for a set of discrete demand scenarios, the expected
#' profit per order quantity as well as the order quantity that is
#' optimal according to the fractile rule.
#'
#' @param nachfrage           vector of possible demand values sorted in
#'                            ascending order (the order quantity candidates)
#' @param wahrscheinlichkeit  vector of the associated probabilities of occurrence
#' @param verkaufspreis       revenue per unit sold (p)
#' @param herstellkosten      cost per unit produced/procured (deliberately
#'                            not called "c", since c() is reserved for vectors in R!)
#' @param restwert            revenue per unsold unit (v)
#' @return list with cost parameters, complete results table and
#'         optimal order quantity
newsvendor_diskret <- function(nachfrage, wahrscheinlichkeit,
                                verkaufspreis, herstellkosten, restwert) {

  # -- 1. Check inputs -------------------------------------------------------
  stopifnot(
    length(nachfrage) == length(wahrscheinlichkeit),
    abs(sum(wahrscheinlichkeit) - 1) < 1e-8,   # probabilities sum to 1
    all(diff(nachfrage) > 0),                   # nachfrage must be sorted in ascending order
    verkaufspreis > herstellkosten,
    herstellkosten > restwert
  )

  # -- 2. Derive cost parameters ---------------------------------------------
  cu <- verkaufspreis - herstellkosten   # underage cost c^u
  co <- herstellkosten - restwert        # overage cost c^o
  cr <- cu / (cu + co)                   # critical ratio

  # -- 3. expected profit for a given order quantity q -----------------------
  # Inner helper function: uses the variables of the enclosing function
  # (cu, co, nachfrage, wahrscheinlichkeit) and is therefore only valid locally.
  gewinn_bei_q <- function(q) {
    gewinn_je_szenario <- cu * pmin(nachfrage, q) - co * pmax(q - nachfrage, 0)
    sum(wahrscheinlichkeit * gewinn_je_szenario)  # expected value
  }

  # -- 4. evaluate all demand scenarios as order quantity candidates ---------
  erwarteter_gewinn <- vapply(nachfrage, gewinn_bei_q, numeric(1))

  # -- 5. cumulative probabilities for the fractile rule ---------------------
  kumuliert <- cumsum(wahrscheinlichkeit)

  # -- 6. optimal order quantity: smallest q with F(q) >= critical ratio -----
  q_stern <- nachfrage[min(which(kumuliert >= cr))]

  # -- 7. return result as a named list --------------------------------------
  list(
    cu             = cu,
    co             = co,
    cr             = cr,
    tabelle        = tibble(
      bestellmenge       = nachfrage,
      wahrscheinlichkeit = wahrscheinlichkeit,
      kumuliert          = kumuliert,
      erwarteter_gewinn  = erwarteter_gewinn
    ),
    q_stern        = q_stern,
    gewinn_optimum = gewinn_bei_q(q_stern)
  )
}

8.4 Discrete demand: the fractile rule

Why does our function choose precisely the smallest order quantity whose cumulative probability \(F(q)\) exceeds a certain threshold? The answer is provided by a marginal analysis: we ask whether it is worth increasing the order quantity by one additional unit.

  • With probability \(1 - F(q)\), actual demand lies above \(q\). In this case the additional unit is sold and avoids a shortage — the expected marginal gain is \(c^u \cdot (1 - F(q))\).
  • With probability \(F(q)\), demand is at most \(q\). In this case the additional unit remains unsold — the expected marginal loss is \(c^o \cdot F(q)\).

It is worth increasing the order quantity as long as the expected marginal gain exceeds the expected marginal loss, i.e. as long as \(c^u \cdot (1-F(q)) > c^o \cdot F(q)\) holds. At the optimum, the marginal gain and marginal loss of the last unit ordered are exactly balanced:

Definition: critical ratio

\[ c^u \cdot \big(1 - F(q^*)\big) = c^o \cdot F(q^*) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad F(q^*) = \underbrace{\frac{c^u}{c^u + c^o}}_{=: CR} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad q^* = F^{-1}(CR). \]

The critical ratio \(CR\) indicates what proportion of the demand distribution should at most be covered by the optimal order quantity. It depends solely on the ratio of underage to overage costs, not on their absolute level.

For our sushi example this gives \(CR = 5/(5+3) = 0.625\). With discrete demand, \(F\) is a step function, so that \(F(q) = CR\) is in general not satisfied exactly for any single \(q\). The correct decision rule is therefore: choose the smallest order quantity \(q\) whose cumulative probability \(F(q)\) reaches at least \(CR\).

Demand \(D\) \(P(D=d)\) \(F(d)\)
30 0.20 0.20
40 0.35 0.55
50 0.30 0.85
60 0.15 1.00

Since \(F(40) = 0.55 < 0.625 \le F(50) = 0.85\), \(q^* = 50\) sushi boxes is the optimal order quantity — exactly the result that our function newsvendor_diskret() already found in the previous section by fully computing all expected profits. This agreement is no coincidence but confirms that the fractile rule and direct computation of the expected profit solve the same optimisation problem — once via a closed-form decision rule, once via complete enumeration.

The critical ratio also allows an intuitive distinction of cases:

  • \(c^u < c^o\): shortages are cheaper than overstocks \(\Rightarrow\) \(CR < 0.5\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(q^*\) lies below the median of demand — it pays to order on the lean side.
  • \(c^u = c^o\): both types of error are equally expensive \(\Rightarrow\) \(CR = 0.5\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(q^*\) equals the median of demand.
  • \(c^u > c^o\): shortages are more expensive than overstocks \(\Rightarrow\) \(CR > 0.5\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(q^*\) lies above the median — it pays to order generously.

In depth (theory): the fractile rule as a first-order condition

For continuous demand with density \(f\) and distribution function \(F\), the marginal analysis can be formulated exactly. The expected costs are \[ E[C(q)] = c^u \int_q^{\infty} (d - q)\, f(d)\, \mathrm{d}d \;+\; c^o \int_{-\infty}^{q} (q - d)\, f(d)\, \mathrm{d}d . \] Differentiating with respect to \(q\) (Leibniz rule: the integrands vanish at the boundary \(d = q\), so only the derivative of the integrands remains) yields \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\,E[C(q)]}{\mathrm{d}q} = -c^u \int_q^{\infty} f(d)\,\mathrm{d}d + c^o \int_{-\infty}^{q} f(d)\,\mathrm{d}d = -c^u\,\big(1 - F(q)\big) + c^o\, F(q). \] Setting this to zero gives \(F(q^*) = c^u/(c^u+c^o)\) – exactly the fractile rule. The second derivative is \((c^u + c^o)\,f(q) \geq 0\), so the expected costs are convex in \(q\), and the first-order condition indeed describes a minimum. The derivative also has an intuitive meaning: \(1 - F(q)\) is the probability that an additional unit is sold, \(F(q)\) the probability that it is left over. The graphs in the lecture, in which the curves of expected shortage and overstock costs intersect or their slopes balance out, are the geometric counterpart of this equation.

8.5 Continuous demand: the normal distribution

In practice, demand is rarely available in the form of a few discrete scenarios but — as discussed in Chapter 7 — is frequently approximated by a continuous distribution, usually the normal distribution \(D \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\). SushiFresh GmbH now supplies six supermarket stores in the Rhine-Main area whose daily demand differs considerably depending on catchment area and store size. In the following we consider the largest of these stores, Frankfurt, for which a daily demand of \(\mu = 280\) boxes with a standard deviation of \(\sigma = 55\) boxes was estimated from the documented sales history (with an unchanged cost structure \(c^u = 5\,\) €, \(c^o = 3\) €, since selling price, production cost and salvage value are product-related rather than store-specific quantities). This is the same store that is also considered in Chapter 7 in the context of the pooling effect on safety stock across the SushiFresh store network.

Since the normal distribution has a strictly monotonically increasing, continuous distribution function \(F\), the inverse function \(F^{-1}\) exists exactly — the fractile rule no longer has to be evaluated approximately via a step function but can be evaluated directly. As in Chapter 7, the standardisation \(Z = (D-\mu)/\sigma \sim N(0,1)\) holds, so that \(q^*\) can be expressed using the quantile of the standard normal distribution returned by qnorm():

\[ q^* = \mu + \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}(CR) = \mu + \sigma \cdot z^*, \qquad z^* = \Phi^{-1}(CR). \]

To also be able to state the expected costs at the optimum, we need the expected values \(E[(D-q)^+]\) and \(E[(q-D)^+]\) for normally distributed demand. Using the standard normal loss function \(L(z) = \varphi(z) - z\cdot(1-\Phi(z))\) — where \(\varphi\) is the density of the standard normal distribution — both expected values can be expressed compactly: \[ E\big[(D-q)^+\big] = \sigma \cdot L(z), \qquad E\big[(q-D)^+\big] = \sigma \cdot L(-z), \qquad z = \frac{q-\mu}{\sigma}. \] Substituting this into the expected cost function and evaluating it at the optimum \(z = z^*\) (where \(\Phi(z^*) = CR\) holds by construction), the terms depending on \(z^*\) cancel each other out, leaving the remarkably simple formula

\[ E\big[C(q^*)\big] = (c^u + c^o) \cdot \sigma \cdot \varphi(z^*). \]

Exactly these two formulae — for \(q^*\) and \(E[C(q^*)]\) — are implemented by the function newsvendor_normal() in R/scm_functions.R, which you already encountered at the beginning of this chapter. Let us recalculate our example:

A methodological digression is permitted at this point: instead of using the formula for \(q^*\) directly, the expected profit \(E[P(q)]\) could also be determined approximately by Monte Carlo simulation — exactly as introduced in Chapter 7. One would draw a very large number of random daily demands with rnorm(), compute the realised profit for each draw according to the profit function \(P(D,q)\) and then average over all replications (for instance with replicate()). For a sufficiently large number of simulation runs, this simulated mean converges to the expected value \(E[P(q)]\) calculated analytically here — a good plausibility test when a closed-form formula is available, as here, and the only practicable solution when it is not.

8.6 Numerical optimisation as an alternative to the closed-form solution

For the normal distribution we were able to determine \(q^*\) conveniently via qnorm() because its distribution function has a known inverse. For many practically relevant distributions — for instance a lognormal, gamma or empirically estimated distribution — no closed form exists for \(F^{-1}\). In such cases numerical optimisation helps, as introduced in the following aside.

R aside: uniroot() and optimize() instead of an analytical solution

The condition \(F(q^*) = CR\) from the fractile rule can be viewed as a root-finding problem: we are looking for the value \(q\) for which the function \(g(q) = F(q) - CR\) becomes zero. The function uniroot() finds exactly such a root within a given interval — without us having to know the inverse function of \(F\) explicitly:

Code
# Objective function: difference between distribution function and critical ratio.
# On success, uniroot() returns the q for which this difference becomes zero.
zielfunktion_wurzel <- function(q) {
  pnorm(q, mean = mu_nachfrage, sd = sigma_nachfrage) - cr
}

loesung_uniroot <- uniroot(
  zielfunktion_wurzel,
  interval = c(mu_nachfrage - 4 * sigma_nachfrage,
               mu_nachfrage + 4 * sigma_nachfrage)
)

loesung_uniroot$root   # should (almost) match q_stern_normal
[1] 297.5252

    function(d) (d - q) * dnorm(d, mean = mu_nachfrage, sd = sigma_nachfrage),

8.7 Critical ratio and service level

The critical ratio \(CR = \Phi(z^*)\) has a second, very practical interpretation: it corresponds exactly to the probability that demand does not exceed the order quantity, i.e. the so-called cycle service level (\(\alpha\) service level) — the proportion of periods in which the entire demand can be met from the available quantity. For our example, \(CR = 0.625\) means: on about 62.5% of all days the order quantity \(q^*\) is sufficient to serve the complete daily demand; on the remaining roughly 37.5% of days a (partial) shortage occurs.

This relationship is the reason why the newsvendor model is important far beyond single-period problems (the lecture emphasises: it is the basis for calculating cost-optimal safety stocks in inventory systems; in Chapter 9 the order-up-to level \(S = \mu(l+1) + z_{CR}\,\sigma\sqrt{l+1}\) is derived from it): in practice, the safety stocks of multi-period inventory systems are very often dimensioned according to exactly this principle — a desired service level is set (e.g. 95%) and the required safety factor is determined from it via \(z = \Phi^{-1}(\text{service level})\), as already hinted at in Chapter 7 in the context of the pooling effect. The only difference from the newsvendor model lies in where the target value of the critical ratio comes from: in the newsvendor model it is derived from costs (\(CR = c^u/(c^u+c^o)\)), whereas in many operational inventory systems it is frequently specified directly as a management decision. It should also be noted that the cycle service level considered here (probability of no shortage) must not be confused with the fill rate, which measures the proportion of demand met in terms of quantity and is as a rule higher than the cycle service level.

8.8 Sensitivity analysis: how does the model respond to changed parameters?

So far we have solved the newsvendor model for fixed values of \(\mu\), \(\sigma\), \(p\), \(c\) and \(v\). In practice, one is often interested in how sensitively the optimal decision reacts to changes in these parameters — for instance when the selling price fluctuates seasonally or the demand estimate is subject to uncertainty. Vectorisation, which you already know from Chapter 5 (sapply()) and Chapter 6 (purrr::map()), is ideal for exactly this: instead of evaluating the model for a single parameter value, we apply it to a whole vector of possible values.

8.9 Areas of application and limitations of the model

The newsvendor model is most useful where smoothing demand through inventory holding is not possible and the cost parameters \(c^u\) and \(c^o\) can be estimated sufficiently well (Thonemann 2023). Typical areas of application are:

  • Seasonal and promotional items (Christmas items, fashion goods for one season), for which reordering within the season is practically ruled out,
  • Fresh goods with a short shelf life (like our sushi example, but also bakery products, flowers or ready meals),
  • Tickets for events or means of transport, whose capacity is fixed on the day of the event or departure,
  • Capacity planning in manufacturing, when capacity can only be set once before the start of a period.

Beyond this, the newsvendor model — as shown in the previous section — is the conceptual basis for calculating cost-optimal safety stocks in multi-period inventory systems, which raises its practical importance far beyond the individual case of a one-off order decision (Silver et al. 2017).

Nevertheless, the model rests on a number of assumptions that need to be examined critically in practice: it is assumed that the demand distribution is known and stationary, that the decision-maker is risk-neutral (i.e. maximises only the expected profit without taking risk aversion towards losses into account), that no fixed ordering costs are incurred and that one order decision actually covers the entire period. Substitution effects between similar products, learning effects from repeated decisions or the interplay with upstream and downstream stages of the supply chain — topics we will encounter again in the next chapter on the bullwhip effect — are not considered in the basic model and may require extensions (Cachon and Terwiesch 2024; Chopra and Meindl 2025).

8.10 Summary

The newsvendor model answers the question of the optimal order quantity for a one-off decision under uncertain demand. The starting point is the underage cost \(c^u = p - c\) and the overage cost \(c^o = c - v\), whose ratio yields the critical ratio \(CR = c^u/(c^u+c^o)\). This corresponds both to the quantile of the demand distribution that determines the optimal order quantity (\(q^* = F^{-1}(CR)\)) and to the economically sensible service level. With discrete demand, the smallest quantity whose cumulative probability reaches at least \(CR\) is chosen; with normally distributed demand, \(q^*\) can be calculated directly via \(\mu + \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}(CR)\). Methodologically, you have learned to encapsulate a complete decision model in your own R function, to search numerically for optimal solutions with uniroot() and optimize() when no closed-form formula is available, and to carry out and visualise sensitivity analyses with vectorised model calls (sapply(), purrr::map_dbl()). These three techniques — custom model functions, numerical optimisation and vectorised sensitivity analyses — will come up again and again in the following chapters.

## Single-period decisions under uncertainty

In Chapter [-@sec-unsicherheit-pooling] we learned how demand uncertainty can be described with probability distributions and how to make statements about normally distributed random variables using `rnorm()`, `dnorm()`, `pnorm()` and `qnorm()`. The principle of Monte Carlo simulation with `replicate()` and the empirical distribution function `ecdf()` were also introduced there. In this chapter we use exactly this toolkit to solve one of the central decision situations of supply chain management: the question of how much of a product should be ordered or produced when demand only becomes known *after* the decision and the quantity can no longer be corrected during the selling period.

This problem is called the **newsvendor model** (also *newsboy problem* or *single-period inventory model*), named after the classic example of a newspaper seller who has to decide every morning how many copies of a daily newspaper to buy before knowing the actual demand of the day. The mathematical structure of the problem was formally studied as early as the 1950s by @arrow1951optimal and has since been one of the most frequently used models in inventory planning [@cachon2024matching; @silver2017inventory]. All applications share the following characteristics:

- **A single order or production decision** is made before the start of the selling period.
- **Within the period** no reordering is possible — supply and demand only finally meet at the end of the period.
- Demand in the period is **uncertain** at the time of the decision, but its distribution is known (e.g. estimated from historical sales data).
- Unsold units have a **lower value** at the end of the period than originally calculated (discount sale, disposal, return to the manufacturer) — and ordering too few units leads to **lost contribution margin**.

This makes the newsvendor model the microeconomic foundation for much more far-reaching questions: How high should the safety stock be in a multi-period warehouse? How large should the capacity of a production line be? How many rooms should a hotel offer for a particular night? We will see in this chapter that all these questions essentially share the same formal structure.

## Case example: sushi boxes in the supermarket

For illustration, we accompany the entire chapter with a running example: **SushiFresh GmbH**, a regional producer of fresh sushi boxes in the Rhine-Main area, supplies several supermarket stores every day with freshly produced sushi boxes. The timeline of a selling day looks as follows for each store:

1. **7 a.m.:** The sushi boxes are freshly prepared and delivered to the respective store before opening.
2. **8 a.m.:** Sales in the supermarket begin. Replenishment during the day is ruled out — the quantity produced is fixed for the whole day.
3. **7 p.m.:** Unsold boxes are collected again by SushiFresh.
4. **8 p.m.:** The leftover goods are sold at a discount, donated or disposed of — regular sale on the following day is no longer permitted for food safety reasons.

The central business decision is made in the morning, long before the actual daily demand of the respective store is known: **How many sushi boxes should SushiFresh produce per store?**

### Revenue and cost structure

The following simplified values apply to a single sushi box:

| Quantity | Meaning | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Selling price $p$ | Revenue per sushi box sold | €10 |
| Production and delivery costs $c$ | Costs per prepared sushi box | €5 |
| Salvage value $v$ | Revenue from evening discount or disposal | €2 |

For economically meaningful applications of the model, $p > c > v$ must always hold: if the salvage value $v$ were not below the production cost $c$, there would be no incentive to limit the order quantity at all — one could produce any amount, since even unsold goods would still be recovered at least at cost. Conversely, if the selling price $p$ were not above the production cost $c$, selling would not be worthwhile in principle.

From these three quantities we derive two central cost categories that will appear again and again throughout the chapter:

::: {.definition}
**Definition: underage and overage costs**

The **underage costs** $c^u$ (*underage cost*) correspond to the lost contribution margin per unit that could not be sold because too little was ordered:
$$c^u = p - c$$

The **overage costs** $c^o$ (*overage cost*) correspond to the loss per unit that was not sold at the end of the period:
$$c^o = c - v$$

For our sushi example this gives: $c^u = 10 - 5 = 5\ \text{€}$ and $c^o = 5 - 2 = 3\ \text{€}$.
:::

Both quantities are *opportunity costs* — no money in the amount of $c^u$ or $c^o$ actually flows out; rather, they represent forgone or lost contribution margin compared with the best possible decision in each case. Precisely this tension between ordering "too little" and "too much" is the core of the newsvendor problem.

## Deriving the profit function

If we denote the (random) daily demand by $D$ and the order quantity set by the supplier by $q$, two situations can be distinguished.

**Case 1 — overage ($q > D$):** More is produced than demanded. Exactly $D$ units are sold, while $q - D$ units must be recovered at the salvage value. The contribution margin is
$$P(D,q) = D \cdot c^u - (q - D) \cdot c^o.$$

**Case 2 — underage ($q < D$):** Less is produced than demanded. Since no more goods are available than were produced, exactly $q$ units are sold — the excess demand $D - q$ remains unserved and the associated contribution margin is lost. We have
$$P(D,q) = q \cdot c^u.$$

Using the *positive part* function $(x)^+ := \max(x,0)$, both cases can be combined into a single profit function valid for any values of $D$ and $q$:
$$
P(D,q) = c^u \cdot \min(D,q) - c^o \cdot (q-D)^+.
$$

Since $\min(D,q) = D - (D-q)^+$ holds (the quantity sold equals demand minus the unserved shortage), the profit function can equivalently be written as:
$$
P(D,q) = \underbrace{D \cdot c^u}_{\text{maximum possible CM}} - \underbrace{(D-q)^+ \cdot c^u}_{\text{shortage costs}} - \underbrace{(q-D)^+ \cdot c^o}_{\text{overstock costs}}.
$$

This notation makes the economic logic particularly clear: starting from the largest possible contribution margin $D \cdot c^u$, which would be achieved if demand were met exactly, two types of cost are deducted — the shortage costs when the order quantity is too low and the overstock costs when it is too high. Since $D$ is a random variable at the time of the decision, we consider the **expected value** over all possible demand realisations:
$$
E[P(q)] = \mu \cdot c^u - c^u \cdot E\big[(D-q)^+\big] - c^o \cdot E\big[(q-D)^+\big],
$$
where $\mu = E[D]$ is the expected demand. Since the first term $\mu \cdot c^u$ does not depend on $q$, maximising the expected profit is equivalent to minimising the expected shortage and overstock costs:
$$
\min_q\ E[C(q)] = c^u \cdot E\big[(D-q)^+\big] + c^o \cdot E\big[(q-D)^+\big].
$$

::: {.fallstudie}
**Case study: expected contribution margin for $q = 40$ boxes (calculation from the lecture)**

Before the optimisation is solved in general, it is worth working through a single production quantity by hand. For a small store, SushiFresh estimates four demand scenarios (30, 40, 50 or 60 boxes with probabilities 0.20 / 0.35 / 0.30 / 0.15). If $q = 40$ boxes are produced, each scenario yields:

| Demand $D$ | $P(D=d)$ | Overstock $(q-D)^+$ | Shortage $(D-q)^+$ | $P(D,q) = c^u\min(D,q) - c^o (q-D)^+$ |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 30 | 0.20 | 10 | 0 | $5\cdot 30 - 3\cdot 10 = 120$ € |
| 40 | 0.35 | 0 | 0 | $5\cdot 40 = 200$ € |
| 50 | 0.30 | 0 | 10 | $5\cdot 40 = 200$ € |
| 60 | 0.15 | 0 | 20 | $5\cdot 40 = 200$ € |

The expected contribution margin is therefore $E[P \mid q=40] = 0.20\cdot 120 + 0.35\cdot 200 + 0.30\cdot 200 + 0.15\cdot 200 = 184$ €.
:::

```{r ch7-funktion-diskret}
#| message: false
library(tidyverse)  # dplyr, tibble, ggplot2, purrr

#' Newsvendor model with discrete demand
#'
#' Calculates, for a set of discrete demand scenarios, the expected
#' profit per order quantity as well as the order quantity that is
#' optimal according to the fractile rule.
#'
#' @param nachfrage           vector of possible demand values sorted in
#'                            ascending order (the order quantity candidates)
#' @param wahrscheinlichkeit  vector of the associated probabilities of occurrence
#' @param verkaufspreis       revenue per unit sold (p)
#' @param herstellkosten      cost per unit produced/procured (deliberately
#'                            not called "c", since c() is reserved for vectors in R!)
#' @param restwert            revenue per unsold unit (v)
#' @return list with cost parameters, complete results table and
#'         optimal order quantity
newsvendor_diskret <- function(nachfrage, wahrscheinlichkeit,
                                verkaufspreis, herstellkosten, restwert) {

  # -- 1. Check inputs -------------------------------------------------------
  stopifnot(
    length(nachfrage) == length(wahrscheinlichkeit),
    abs(sum(wahrscheinlichkeit) - 1) < 1e-8,   # probabilities sum to 1
    all(diff(nachfrage) > 0),                   # nachfrage must be sorted in ascending order
    verkaufspreis > herstellkosten,
    herstellkosten > restwert
  )

  # -- 2. Derive cost parameters ---------------------------------------------
  cu <- verkaufspreis - herstellkosten   # underage cost c^u
  co <- herstellkosten - restwert        # overage cost c^o
  cr <- cu / (cu + co)                   # critical ratio

  # -- 3. expected profit for a given order quantity q -----------------------
  # Inner helper function: uses the variables of the enclosing function
  # (cu, co, nachfrage, wahrscheinlichkeit) and is therefore only valid locally.
  gewinn_bei_q <- function(q) {
    gewinn_je_szenario <- cu * pmin(nachfrage, q) - co * pmax(q - nachfrage, 0)
    sum(wahrscheinlichkeit * gewinn_je_szenario)  # expected value
  }

  # -- 4. evaluate all demand scenarios as order quantity candidates ---------
  erwarteter_gewinn <- vapply(nachfrage, gewinn_bei_q, numeric(1))

  # -- 5. cumulative probabilities for the fractile rule ---------------------
  kumuliert <- cumsum(wahrscheinlichkeit)

  # -- 6. optimal order quantity: smallest q with F(q) >= critical ratio -----
  q_stern <- nachfrage[min(which(kumuliert >= cr))]

  # -- 7. return result as a named list --------------------------------------
  list(
    cu             = cu,
    co             = co,
    cr             = cr,
    tabelle        = tibble(
      bestellmenge       = nachfrage,
      wahrscheinlichkeit = wahrscheinlichkeit,
      kumuliert          = kumuliert,
      erwarteter_gewinn  = erwarteter_gewinn
    ),
    q_stern        = q_stern,
    gewinn_optimum = gewinn_bei_q(q_stern)
  )
}
```

## Discrete demand: the fractile rule

Why does our function choose precisely the smallest order quantity whose cumulative probability $F(q)$ exceeds a certain threshold? The answer is provided by a **marginal analysis**: we ask whether it is worth increasing the order quantity by one additional unit.

- With probability $1 - F(q)$, actual demand lies *above* $q$. In this case the additional unit is sold and avoids a shortage — the expected marginal gain is $c^u \cdot (1 - F(q))$.
- With probability $F(q)$, demand is at most $q$. In this case the additional unit remains unsold — the expected marginal loss is $c^o \cdot F(q)$.

It is worth increasing the order quantity as long as the expected marginal gain exceeds the expected marginal loss, i.e. as long as $c^u \cdot (1-F(q)) > c^o \cdot F(q)$ holds. At the **optimum**, the marginal gain and marginal loss of the last unit ordered are exactly balanced:

::: {.definition}
**Definition: critical ratio**

$$
c^u \cdot \big(1 - F(q^*)\big) = c^o \cdot F(q^*) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad F(q^*) = \underbrace{\frac{c^u}{c^u + c^o}}_{=: CR} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad q^* = F^{-1}(CR).
$$

The **critical ratio** $CR$ indicates what proportion of the demand distribution should at most be covered by the optimal order quantity. It depends solely on the *ratio* of underage to overage costs, not on their absolute level.
:::

For our sushi example this gives $CR = 5/(5+3) = 0.625$. With **discrete** demand, $F$ is a step function, so that $F(q) = CR$ is in general not satisfied exactly for any single $q$. The correct decision rule is therefore: choose the **smallest** order quantity $q$ whose cumulative probability $F(q)$ reaches at least $CR$.

| Demand $D$ | $P(D=d)$ | $F(d)$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 30 | 0.20 | 0.20 |
| 40 | 0.35 | 0.55 |
| 50 | 0.30 | 0.85 |
| 60 | 0.15 | 1.00 |

Since $F(40) = 0.55 < 0.625 \le F(50) = 0.85$, $q^* = 50$ sushi boxes is the optimal order quantity — exactly the result that our function `newsvendor_diskret()` already found in the previous section by fully computing all expected profits. This agreement is no coincidence but confirms that the fractile rule and direct computation of the expected profit solve the *same* optimisation problem — once via a closed-form decision rule, once via complete enumeration.

The critical ratio also allows an intuitive distinction of cases:

- **$c^u < c^o$:** shortages are cheaper than overstocks $\Rightarrow$ $CR < 0.5$ $\Rightarrow$ $q^*$ lies **below** the median of demand — it pays to order on the lean side.
- **$c^u = c^o$:** both types of error are equally expensive $\Rightarrow$ $CR = 0.5$ $\Rightarrow$ $q^*$ equals the **median** of demand.
- **$c^u > c^o$:** shortages are more expensive than overstocks $\Rightarrow$ $CR > 0.5$ $\Rightarrow$ $q^*$ lies **above** the median — it pays to order generously.

::: {.vertiefung}
**In depth (theory): the fractile rule as a first-order condition**

For continuous demand with density $f$ and distribution function $F$, the marginal analysis can be formulated exactly. The expected costs are
$$
E[C(q)] = c^u \int_q^{\infty} (d - q)\, f(d)\, \mathrm{d}d \;+\; c^o \int_{-\infty}^{q} (q - d)\, f(d)\, \mathrm{d}d .
$$
Differentiating with respect to $q$ (Leibniz rule: the integrands vanish at the boundary $d = q$, so only the derivative of the integrands remains) yields
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\,E[C(q)]}{\mathrm{d}q} = -c^u \int_q^{\infty} f(d)\,\mathrm{d}d + c^o \int_{-\infty}^{q} f(d)\,\mathrm{d}d = -c^u\,\big(1 - F(q)\big) + c^o\, F(q).
$$
Setting this to zero gives $F(q^*) = c^u/(c^u+c^o)$ – exactly the fractile rule. The second derivative is $(c^u + c^o)\,f(q) \geq 0$, so the expected costs are **convex** in $q$, and the first-order condition indeed describes a minimum. The derivative also has an intuitive meaning: $1 - F(q)$ is the probability that an additional unit is sold, $F(q)$ the probability that it is left over. The graphs in the lecture, in which the curves of expected shortage and overstock costs intersect or their slopes balance out, are the geometric counterpart of this equation.
:::

## Continuous demand: the normal distribution

In practice, demand is rarely available in the form of a few discrete scenarios but — as discussed in Chapter [-@sec-unsicherheit-pooling] — is frequently approximated by a continuous distribution, usually the normal distribution $D \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$. SushiFresh GmbH now supplies six supermarket stores in the Rhine-Main area whose daily demand differs considerably depending on catchment area and store size. In the following we consider the largest of these stores, **Frankfurt**, for which a daily demand of $\mu = 280$ boxes with a standard deviation of $\sigma = 55$ boxes was estimated from the documented sales history (with an unchanged cost structure $c^u = 5\,$ €, $c^o = 3$ €, since selling price, production cost and salvage value are product-related rather than store-specific quantities). This is the same store that is also considered in Chapter [-@sec-unsicherheit-pooling] in the context of the pooling effect on safety stock across the SushiFresh store network.

Since the normal distribution has a strictly monotonically increasing, continuous distribution function $F$, the inverse function $F^{-1}$ exists exactly — the fractile rule no longer has to be evaluated approximately via a step function but can be evaluated directly. As in Chapter [-@sec-unsicherheit-pooling], the standardisation $Z = (D-\mu)/\sigma \sim N(0,1)$ holds, so that $q^*$ can be expressed using the quantile of the standard normal distribution returned by `qnorm()`:

$$
q^* = \mu + \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}(CR) = \mu + \sigma \cdot z^*, \qquad z^* = \Phi^{-1}(CR).
$$

To also be able to state the expected costs at the optimum, we need the expected values $E[(D-q)^+]$ and $E[(q-D)^+]$ for normally distributed demand. Using the **standard normal loss function** $L(z) = \varphi(z) - z\cdot(1-\Phi(z))$ — where $\varphi$ is the density of the standard normal distribution — both expected values can be expressed compactly:
$$
E\big[(D-q)^+\big] = \sigma \cdot L(z), \qquad E\big[(q-D)^+\big] = \sigma \cdot L(-z), \qquad z = \frac{q-\mu}{\sigma}.
$$
Substituting this into the expected cost function and evaluating it at the optimum $z = z^*$ (where $\Phi(z^*) = CR$ holds by construction), the terms depending on $z^*$ cancel each other out, leaving the remarkably simple formula

$$
E\big[C(q^*)\big] = (c^u + c^o) \cdot \sigma \cdot \varphi(z^*).
$$


Exactly these two formulae — for $q^*$ and $E[C(q^*)]$ — are implemented by the function `newsvendor_normal()` in `R/scm_functions.R`, which you already encountered at the beginning of this chapter. Let us recalculate our example:

```{r ch7-normal-parameter}

co <- 3   # overage cost

# expected shortage and overstock costs at the optimum

```

With $CR = 0.625$ we obtain $z^* = \Phi^{-1}(0.625) \approx 0.319$ and thus $q^* = 280 + 55 \cdot 0.319 \approx 297.5$ sushi boxes. Since only whole boxes can be produced, in practice one would round up to $q^* = 298$. The expected shortage and overstock costs at the optimum amount to around €166.8 per day, so that the expected profit is $E[P(q^*)] = \mu \cdot c^u - E[C(q^*)] \approx 280 \cdot 5 - 166.8 \approx 1{,}233.2\,$€ per day.

A methodological digression is permitted at this point: instead of using the formula for $q^*$ directly, the expected profit $E[P(q)]$ could also be determined approximately by **Monte Carlo simulation** — exactly as introduced in Chapter [-@sec-unsicherheit-pooling]. One would draw a very large number of random daily demands with `rnorm()`, compute the realised profit for each draw according to the profit function $P(D,q)$ and then average over all replications (for instance with `replicate()`). For a sufficiently large number of simulation runs, this simulated mean converges to the expected value $E[P(q)]$ calculated analytically here — a good plausibility test when a closed-form formula is available, as here, and the only practicable solution when it is not.

## Numerical optimisation as an alternative to the closed-form solution

For the normal distribution we were able to determine $q^*$ conveniently via `qnorm()` because its distribution function has a known inverse. For many practically relevant distributions — for instance a lognormal, gamma or empirically estimated distribution — no closed form exists for $F^{-1}$. In such cases numerical **optimisation** helps, as introduced in the following aside.

::: {.r-exkurs}
**R aside: `uniroot()` and `optimize()` instead of an analytical solution**

The condition $F(q^*) = CR$ from the fractile rule can be viewed as a **root-finding problem**: we are looking for the value $q$ for which the function $g(q) = F(q) - CR$ becomes zero. The function `uniroot()` finds exactly such a root within a given interval — without us having to know the inverse function of $F$ explicitly:

```{r ch7-uniroot}
# Objective function: difference between distribution function and critical ratio.
# On success, uniroot() returns the q for which this difference becomes zero.
zielfunktion_wurzel <- function(q) {
  pnorm(q, mean = mu_nachfrage, sd = sigma_nachfrage) - cr
}

loesung_uniroot <- uniroot(
  zielfunktion_wurzel,
  interval = c(mu_nachfrage - 4 * sigma_nachfrage,
               mu_nachfrage + 4 * sigma_nachfrage)
)

loesung_uniroot$root   # should (almost) match q_stern_normal
```

Alternatively, the expected cost function $E[C(q)]$ can also be **minimised directly** without the detour via the fractile rule — useful, for instance, when only the cost function is known but not its analytical minimum. `integrate()` computes the expected values $E[(D-q)^+]$ and $E[(q-D)^+]$ numerically, and `optimize()` then searches for the minimum of the total cost function:

```{r ch7-optimize}
erwartete_kosten <- function(q) {
  e_fehlmenge <- integrate(
    function(d) (d - q) * dnorm(d, mean = mu_nachfrage, sd = sigma_nachfrage),
    lower = q, upper = mu_nachfrage + 8 * sigma_nachfrage
  )$value

  e_ueberbestand <- integrate(
    function(d) (q - d) * dnorm(d, mean = mu_nachfrage, sd = sigma_nachfrage),
    lower = mu_nachfrage - 8 * sigma_nachfrage, upper = q
  )$value

  cu * e_fehlmenge + co * e_ueberbestand
}

loesung_optimize <- optimize(
  erwartete_kosten,
  interval = c(mu_nachfrage - 4 * sigma_nachfrage,
               mu_nachfrage + 4 * sigma_nachfrage)
)

loesung_optimize$minimum   # optimal order quantity
loesung_optimize$objective # minimum expected costs
```

Both numerical methods yield (apart from minimal rounding deviations) the same result as the closed-form formula $q^* = \mu + \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}(CR)$. The advantage of this approach becomes apparent as soon as `dnorm()`/`pnorm()` are replaced by the density or distribution function of any other distribution — the structure of the code remains unchanged, whereas an analytical derivation would have to be carried out anew for each new distribution.
:::

## Critical ratio and service level

The critical ratio $CR = \Phi(z^*)$ has a second, very practical interpretation: it corresponds exactly to the probability that demand does **not** exceed the order quantity, i.e. the so-called **cycle service level** ($\alpha$ service level) — the proportion of periods in which the entire demand can be met from the available quantity. For our example, $CR = 0.625$ means: on about 62.5% of all days the order quantity $q^*$ is sufficient to serve the complete daily demand; on the remaining roughly 37.5% of days a (partial) shortage occurs.

This relationship is the reason why the newsvendor model is important far beyond single-period problems (the lecture emphasises: it is the basis for calculating cost-optimal safety stocks in inventory systems; in Chapter [-@sec-bullwhip] the order-up-to level $S = \mu(l+1) + z_{CR}\,\sigma\sqrt{l+1}$ is derived from it): in practice, the **safety stocks** of multi-period inventory systems are very often dimensioned according to exactly this principle — a desired service level is set (e.g. 95%) and the required safety factor is determined from it via $z = \Phi^{-1}(\text{service level})$, as already hinted at in Chapter [-@sec-unsicherheit-pooling] in the context of the pooling effect. The only difference from the newsvendor model lies in *where* the target value of the critical ratio comes from: in the newsvendor model it is derived from costs ($CR = c^u/(c^u+c^o)$), whereas in many operational inventory systems it is frequently specified directly as a management decision. It should also be noted that the cycle service level considered here (probability of *no* shortage) must not be confused with the **fill rate**, which measures the proportion of demand met *in terms of quantity* and is as a rule higher than the cycle service level.

## Sensitivity analysis: how does the model respond to changed parameters?

So far we have solved the newsvendor model for fixed values of $\mu$, $\sigma$, $p$, $c$ and $v$. In practice, one is often interested in **how sensitively** the optimal decision reacts to changes in these parameters — for instance when the selling price fluctuates seasonally or the demand estimate is subject to uncertainty. **Vectorisation**, which you already know from Chapter [-@sec-standort-1] (`sapply()`) and Chapter [-@sec-standort-2] (`purrr::map()`), is ideal for exactly this: instead of evaluating the model for a single parameter value, we apply it to a whole vector of possible values.

::: {.r-exkurs}
**R aside: sensitivity analysis through vectorisation**

First, we examine how the expected profit $E[P(q)]$ behaves over a fine grid of possible order quantities — for the Frankfurt store, i.e. for every quantity between 100 and 460 boxes. To do so, we encapsulate the profit formula from the normal distribution case in a small function and evaluate it with `sapply()` for each grid point:

```{r}
#| label: fig-newsvendor-sensitivitaet-q
#| fig-cap: "Expected profit as a function of the order quantity q"
#| fig-width: 7
#| fig-height: 4

# Model function: expected profit for a given q (normal distribution case)
erwarteter_gewinn_normal <- function(q, mu, sigma, cu, co) {
  z <- (q - mu) / sigma
  L <- function(z) dnorm(z) - z * (1 - pnorm(z))  # standard normal loss function
  e_fehlmenge    <- sigma * L(z)
  e_ueberbestand <- sigma * L(-z)
  mu * cu - cu * e_fehlmenge - co * e_ueberbestand
}

q_raster <- seq(100, 460, by = 2)

# sapply() applies the function to each value of the grid
gewinn_je_q <- sapply(
  q_raster, erwarteter_gewinn_normal,
  mu = mu_nachfrage, sigma = sigma_nachfrage, cu = cu, co = co
)

sensitivitaet_q <- tibble(bestellmenge = q_raster, erwarteter_gewinn = gewinn_je_q)

ggplot(sensitivitaet_q, aes(x = bestellmenge, y = erwarteter_gewinn)) +
  geom_line(colour = "#0B6E4F", linewidth = 1) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = q_stern_normal, linetype = "dashed", colour = "#D97B29") +
  labs(
    x = "Order quantity q", y = "Expected profit E[P(q)] in €",
    title = "Sensitivity of expected profit to the order quantity"
  ) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13)
```

The curve vividly confirms what we had already derived analytically: the expected profit is a **concave** function of the order quantity with a unique maximum at $q^*$ — both significantly smaller and significantly larger quantities reduce the expected profit.

In a second step, we examine how much the *optimal* order quantity itself changes when the selling price $p$ changes (e.g. due to seasonal discount promotions). For this we use `purrr::map_dbl()`, which — analogous to `sapply()` — iterates a function over a vector and is guaranteed to return a numeric vector:


The graph shows an intuitive relationship: if the selling price $p$ rises, the underage cost $c^u = p - c$ rises and thus also the critical ratio $CR$ — the supplier should therefore tend to order **more**, because a shortage has now become more expensive. In practice, such sensitivity analyses are often at least as valuable as the single optimal value itself, because they show how robust a decision is to estimation errors or market fluctuations.

## Areas of application and limitations of the model

The newsvendor model is most useful where **smoothing demand through inventory holding is not possible** and the cost parameters $c^u$ and $c^o$ can be estimated sufficiently well [@thonemann2023operations]. Typical areas of application are:

- **Seasonal and promotional items** (Christmas items, fashion goods for one season), for which reordering within the season is practically ruled out,
- **Fresh goods with a short shelf life** (like our sushi example, but also bakery products, flowers or ready meals),
- **Tickets for events or means of transport**, whose capacity is fixed on the day of the event or departure,
- **Capacity planning** in manufacturing, when capacity can only be set once before the start of a period.

Beyond this, the newsvendor model — as shown in the previous section — is the conceptual basis for calculating cost-optimal safety stocks in multi-period inventory systems, which raises its practical importance far beyond the individual case of a one-off order decision [@silver2017inventory].

Nevertheless, the model rests on a number of assumptions that need to be examined critically in practice: it is assumed that the demand distribution is **known and stationary**, that the decision-maker is **risk-neutral** (i.e. maximises only the *expected* profit without taking risk aversion towards losses into account), that no **fixed ordering costs** are incurred and that **one** order decision actually covers the entire period. Substitution effects between similar products, learning effects from repeated decisions or the interplay with upstream and downstream stages of the supply chain — topics we will encounter again in the next chapter on the bullwhip effect — are not considered in the basic model and may require extensions [@cachon2024matching; @chopra2025supply].

## Summary

The newsvendor model answers the question of the optimal order quantity for a one-off decision under uncertain demand. The starting point is the underage cost $c^u = p - c$ and the overage cost $c^o = c - v$, whose ratio yields the critical ratio $CR = c^u/(c^u+c^o)$. This corresponds both to the quantile of the demand distribution that determines the optimal order quantity ($q^* = F^{-1}(CR)$) and to the economically sensible service level. With discrete demand, the smallest quantity whose cumulative probability reaches at least $CR$ is chosen; with normally distributed demand, $q^*$ can be calculated directly via $\mu + \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}(CR)$. Methodologically, you have learned to encapsulate a complete decision model in your own R function, to search numerically for optimal solutions with `uniroot()` and `optimize()` when no closed-form formula is available, and to carry out and visualise sensitivity analyses with vectorised model calls (`sapply()`, `purrr::map_dbl()`). These three techniques — custom model functions, numerical optimisation and vectorised sensitivity analyses — will come up again and again in the following chapters.
